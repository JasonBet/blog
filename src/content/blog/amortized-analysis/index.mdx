The first time someone tells you that appending to a dynamic array is $O(1)$
amortised, it sounds like a dodge. The operation is obviously not $O(1)$ — some
appends copy the entire array. What the claim actually says is stronger and
more precise than "usually fast", and it is worth being able to state exactly.

<Callout type="definition" title="Amortised cost">
  An operation has amortised cost $T$ if any sequence of $n$ such operations,
  starting from an empty structure, costs at most $n \cdot T$ in total. This is
  a worst-case statement about sequences — not an average, and not a
  probabilistic claim.
</Callout>

That distinction matters. Average-case analysis assumes a distribution over
inputs. Amortised analysis assumes nothing: an adversary picks the worst
sequence it can, and the bound still holds.

## Method 1: aggregate

Add up the total cost of $n$ operations, then divide. For a dynamic array that
doubles its capacity when full, the copies happen at sizes
$1, 2, 4, \ldots, 2^k$ where $2^k < n$, so the total copying work is

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor} 2^i < 2n
$$

Each append also does $O(1)$ work of its own, so $n$ appends cost less than
$3n$ in total, and the amortised cost per append is $O(1)$.

This is the method to reach for when the expensive operations are rare and
their cost forms a series you can actually sum. It gives you one number for the
whole sequence, which is also its weakness: it says nothing about individual
operation types.

## Method 2: the accounting (banker's) method

Charge each operation more than it costs and bank the surplus as credit, then
spend that credit on the expensive operations. The bound holds as long as the
balance never goes negative.

For the dynamic array, charge 3 units per append:

| Unit | What it pays for |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Writing the new element |
| 1 | Eventually copying this element on the next resize |
| 1 | Eventually copying one older element that has no credit left |

When the array of size $m$ fills up, the $m/2$ elements added since the last
resize each carry 2 units of stored credit — exactly enough to move all $m$
elements. The balance touches zero and never crosses it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Choosing the charge">
  Start by writing down the expensive operation, count what it has to pay for,
  then work backwards to how many cheap operations must have preceded it. The
  charge is that ratio, rounded up.
</Callout>

## Method 3: the potential method

Define a potential function $\Phi$ mapping each state of the structure to a
non-negative real, with $\Phi(D_0) = 0$. The amortised cost of an operation is
its real cost plus the change in potential:

$$
\hat{c_i} = c_i + \Phi(D_i) - \Phi(D_{i-1})
$$

Sum over $n$ operations and the middle terms telescope:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{c_i} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i + \Phi(D_n) - \Phi(D_0)
$$

Since $\Phi(D_n) \geq 0$ and $\Phi(D_0) = 0$, the amortised total is an upper
bound on the real total. For the dynamic array with $s$ items and capacity $m$,
take

$$
\Phi = 2s - m
$$

A cheap append raises $s$ by one and leaves $m$ alone, so $\Phi$ rises by 2 and
the amortised cost is $1 + 2 = 3$. A resizing append copies $m$ items but
halves the potential in exactly the right way, and the amortised cost comes out
to 3 again.

## Which method to use

```text
Rare, summable spikes         -> aggregate
Several operation types       -> accounting
State-dependent cost          -> potential
```

The potential method subsumes the other two — the accounting method's bank
balance *is* a potential function — but it demands that you guess $\Phi$, and a
bad guess tells you nothing. In practice I try aggregate first because it is
concrete, and reach for potential when the cost of an operation depends on how
unbalanced the structure currently is. Splay trees are the canonical case:
there is no useful way to talk about them without a potential function.

## The trap worth naming

Amortised $O(1)$ says nothing about any *individual* operation. That single
resizing append really is $\Theta(n)$, and if you are writing anything with a
latency budget — a game loop, an audio callback, a real-time controller — the
amortised bound is the wrong bound to be reasoning about. You want the worst
case per operation, and you will probably want to pay for it with an
incremental-resize scheme that copies a few elements on every append instead of
all of them at once.

Amortised analysis is a statement about throughput. Do not let it quietly
become a claim about latency.
