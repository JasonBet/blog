Short one. Our complexity course spent a lecture on what the P vs NP question
actually says, and I discovered I had been carrying around three
misconceptions. Writing them down so they stay corrected.

## It is not "can computers solve hard problems"

The question is about a specific and rather narrow thing: whether every
decision problem whose *solutions can be verified* in polynomial time can also
be *solved* in polynomial time. That is it. It says nothing about
approximation, nothing about average-case difficulty, and nothing about what is
practical.

<Callout type="definition" title="The classes">
  **P** — decision problems solvable by a deterministic Turing machine in
  polynomial time. **NP** — decision problems whose *yes* instances have a
  certificate verifiable in polynomial time. P ⊆ NP is immediate; the open
  question is whether the containment is strict.
</Callout>

## "NP" does not stand for "non-polynomial"

It stands for *nondeterministic polynomial time*. This one is embarrassing in
retrospect, but the wrong expansion is common enough that it survives
undisturbed for years. The nondeterminism is the guessing: an NP machine may
guess a certificate and then verify it deterministically in polynomial time.

The consequence of getting this wrong is believing that NP-hard means
"exponential", which then makes NP-completeness results sound like lower-bound
theorems. They are not. They are reductions.

## NP-complete does not mean "hopeless in practice"

SAT is the canonical NP-complete problem, and modern SAT solvers routinely
dispatch industrial instances with millions of clauses. The hardness is a
worst-case statement over all instances; real instances have structure, and
solvers exploit it ruthlessly.

The practical takeaway my professor kept repeating: NP-completeness tells you
not to look for a general polynomial algorithm. It does not tell you your
particular instances are hard.

## Why P ≠ NP is expected but unproven

The near-universal expectation is that P ≠ NP. What is genuinely surprising is
how thoroughly the obvious proof strategies have been ruled out:

- **Relativisation** (Baker–Gill–Solovay, 1975) — there exist oracles $A$ and
  $B$ with $P^A = NP^A$ and $P^B \neq NP^B$. Any proof that survives adding an
  oracle cannot settle the question either way, which eliminates ordinary
  diagonalisation.
- **Natural proofs** (Razborov–Rudich, 1994) — a large class of
  circuit-lower-bound arguments would, if they worked, break the
  pseudorandom generators most of cryptography assumes exist.
- **Algebrisation** (Aaronson–Wigderson, 2008) — extends the relativisation
  barrier to cover the algebraic techniques that got around it.

So the honest state of things is not "nobody has been clever enough yet". It is
"the three families of technique we know about have each been proved
insufficient". That is a much stranger situation, and a much more interesting
one.
